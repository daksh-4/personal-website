Whether it be the Odyssey or the Vedas, it was the job of scholars to memorise epic tales, passed down generation to generation through word of mouth. Very few could read and write so information could be lost in a generation if not preserved appropriately. Now vast quantities of information are organised and available 24/7. While the way we access content has changed, the bones of good learning practices are the same. Spaced repetition and active recall.

  1. Read a fact or perform a task for the first time.
  2. Wait some time
  3. Try to recreate what you did or read or saw the first time from memory.
  4. Inevitably fail but see what you were missing.
  5. Back to step 2.

Broadly this calls on three states? (not sure if states is the right word). Recalling, waiting and reviewing. And at any point when trying to learn we are in one of these states whether consciously or subconsciously.

This seems almost too obvious to deserve an essay. And yet an enormous industry has grown around trying to make learning faster. Books and courses that vary just enough between them to leave readers dissatisfied and still looking for a magic bullet. Is this even something worth chasing?

An Affliction of Optimisation

There is an idea that learning is something to be optimised and discretised. Are you a visual learner or a kinesthetic learner? ( this concept of learning types has been thoroughly disproven) In my opinion, any one way of learning can be just as good as another so long as it incorporates the three steps of recalling, waiting and reviewing. In fact the thing that ended up improving the rate of learning was probably to do with becoming more intentional with the process and paying attention to what is in front of you. The shortest possible guide on all you need to know about learning is a thin book called “ Make It Stick” by Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III and Mark A. McDaniel. To make things clear, I would never discourage reading more about different topics, but doing so solely for the pursuit of extra learning gains will lead to diminishing returns.

Trying to optimise learning is counter-intuitive after a certain point. The process should be effortful (there is a nuance between effortful and difficult), you are meant to keep forgetting things. Trying to remember everything you read once is simply not realistic and any time spent chasing this could have been spent doing other things. In my very anecdotal, non scientific wishy washy experience you probably retain somewhere between 10 to 70 percent of what you learn depending on the type of content (and the confidence interval on this is very large). Point being, not only do you have minimal control on retention outside of focus and ensuring your learning material is at the right level, it is meaningless to try and predict how much of something you are going to forget. The only solution is to revisit the concept after some time and try again.

What is the point of learning this thing if eventually I am just going to forget it? I love talking and thinking about this. Again very much straying into the realm of anecdotal evidence, but there is an intangible feeling I get when I understand a new concept for the first time. A new connection has formed between ideas that were previously unrelated. Perhaps that is what I actually mean when I say that I have learned something. Not that I have successfully stored another fact in my head, but that I have acquired another way of looking at things. And the details will fade, but the appreciation does not. Like a map, I may forget the name of a town I once visited, but the shape of the landscape remains. I’d like to give you an example here but I suspect some of the most important things I have learnt are things I could no longer explain particularly well. They have been absorbed into the way I think. Tied into the web of assumptions, intuitions, analogies and connections rather than discrete pieces of information. And when you revisit a connection or add something new – plucking a strand of information – the adjacent section of webbing resonates. Consolidating ideas requires you to revisit the surrounding context, providing a positive feedback loop of recollection. I think maybe if I was too concerned with my retention percentages I might miss this.

Low Yield Learning

Will this come up on the test? Is an incredibly useful question if your only objective is to pass the test, but meaningless if the objective is to become an educated person. When learning becomes synonymous with test-taking, there is an inevitable focus on “worth” of knowledge and skills.

There is another way to think about high yield. If you treat day to day life as an active recall process, then what you repeatedly encounter becomes high yield by definition. Where does low yield learning come into this? I recently watched a video by Hank Green in which he describes knowledge and skills as something like a personal junk drawer. The miscellaneous, seemingly unrelated, paraphernalia picked up by exploring and reading outside of your most commonly used brain pathways may eventually turn out to be exactly what you need. A biology student learning about evolutionary theory might later recognise selection-like processes in an entirely different system. The usefulness of the knowledge is therefore impossible to measure at the moment it is acquired. You cannot know which conceptual tools you will eventually need, because you cannot predict the problems you will eventually encounter.

There is also an issue with high yield skills is that everyone knows them. If you work as a teacher there may be core competencies that are shared by your colleagues. Accreditation exams exist partly to establish a common baseline across a profession. But they do not explain the enormous differences between people who possess the same baseline of knowledge. Things that initially seemed irrelevant become our personal flair that set us apart as individuals. Steve Jobs famously described taking a calligraphy course at Reed College as something that later influenced the typography and visual design of the Macintosh. At the time, there was no obvious reason for a future computer entrepreneur to learn calligraphy. It was, in the language of modern education, profoundly low yield.

LLMs

Somehow large language models make their way into every conversation. I promise I do have a point to make here and am not just drawing this in for the sake of it. I don’t have a fully concrete understanding of model training but what I can say is that models “learn” in a probabilistic manner. We can re-define our learning steps set out in the introduction to apply to machines as follows (in a very generalised way):

  1. Receive an input and make a prediction
  2. Compare the prediction against the correct answer
  3. Quantify the difference using a loss function
  4. Adjust the internal parameters through backpropogation
  5. Back to step 1

Consequently, it becomes very good at reproducing statistical regularities in language. If we accept, at least provisionally, that machines are particularly good at learning patterns from existing information, then perhaps one of the things that distinguished human intellectual development is the ability to make unexpected connections between seemingly unrelated areas of knowledge 1. This idea is formalised in the paper “ https://www.tomzahavy.com/projects/llms-cant-jump” by Tom Zahavy at Google Deepmind. He distinguishes between three forms of reasoning: induction, deduction and abduction. Induction involves identifying patterns from individual observations. Deduction involves applying established principles to reach specific conclusions. Abduction is something rather different: the generation of new explanatory hypotheses. It is the ability to look at something you observe and propose an entirely new way of understanding it. Without exploring the learning space fully, you might be limiting the opportunities to make connections with ideas outside that domain.

Conclusions?

This essay reads more like a rant, but that was really not my intention. I thought the concept of “low yield learning” (as defined by me to be the pursuit of learning information without paying heed to it’s future utility) to have lots of interesting implications for self-motivated learners. I hope the reader enjoys this idea and in the spirit of their new found willingness to explore might read some of the other essays on my website?
