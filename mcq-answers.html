1 Hypersensitivity reactions in SLE

Answer: b. Type II

The vignette describes antiphospholipid syndrome, which is secondary to SLE here. The antibodies against beta 2-glycoprotein I and cardiolipin bind their target antigens where those antigens sit on cell surfaces, most importantly on platelets and vascular endothelium. Binding activates those cells directly, producing the the prothrombotic state responsible for her deep vein thrombosis and her recurrent miscarriages. Antibody directed against a cell-surface antigen is a type II reaction.

The prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time is a laboratory artefact rather than evidence of bleeding risk. The antibodies interfere with the phospholipid reagent used in the assay, so the number goes up while the patient clots more, not less. A normal bleeding time alongside it is the clue that this is not a genuine coagulopathy.

Type III is the tempting distractor, because SLE itself is the textbook immune complex disease and is responsible for the glomerulonephritis, arthritis and malar rash seen elsewhere in the condition. The question asks specifically about the mechanism behind the current presentation, and thrombosis in antiphospholipid syndrome is not caused by complex deposition. Type I would require IgE and mast cell degranulation (think typical allergic reaction), and type IV would be T cell mediated and delayed (think contact dermatitis) neither of which fits.

2 Retinal haemorrhages in the neonate

Answer: c. Vitamin K deficiency

This neonate has vitamin K deficiency bleeding. On a preclinical exam, instead you might see the answer be “Reduced synthesis of gamma-carboxylated clotting factors” instead. Newborns have low vitamin K stores, and breast milk contains relatively little vitamin K. Vitamin K is required for gamma-carboxylation of clotting factors II, VII, IX and X (and also the anticoagulants protein C and S), allowing them to bind calcium and participate effectively in coagulation.

The persistent bleeding from the umbilical stump is an important clue to a coagulation disorder. The retinal haemorrhages and ecchymoses could suggest non-accidental trauma, but in this context they can occur as manifestations of a bleeding disorder.

Factor VIII deficiency (A) causes haemophilia A and typically presents with deep tissue and joint bleeding rather than this pattern in a neonate. Maternal antibodies against platelet glycoproteins (B) cause neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia, which would cause bleeding due to thrombocytopenia rather than impaired coagulation. DIC (D) would generally occur in the context of an underlying condition such as severe infection and involves consumption of both clotting factors and platelets. Shaken baby syndrome (E) can cause retinal haemorrhages, but it does not explain persistent bleeding from the umbilical stump.

3 Erythropoietin therapy

Answer: d. Microcytic, hypochromic red blood cells

Chronic kidney disease commonly causes normocytic, normochromic anaemia because damaged kidneys produce less erythropoietin (EPO). EPO is normally produced predominantly by specialised interstitial fibroblast-like cells in the renal cortex and outer medulla. When renal tissue senses reduced oxygen delivery, these cells increase EPO production. EPO travels through the circulation to the bone marrow, where it stimulates erythroid precursor cells and promotes red blood cell production.

In CKD, progressive renal damage impairs the function of these EPO-producing cells. Consequently, EPO production falls despite the presence of anaemia, resulting in inadequate stimulation of the bone marrow and reduced red blood cell production. The resulting anaemia is typically normocytic (red cells are of normal size tested by mean corpuscular volume) and normochromic (red cells have a normal concentration of haemoglobin tested by mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration).

Therefore, the initial anaemia of CKD is generally characterised by a reduced number of red blood cells rather than an abnormality in their size or haemoglobin content. Treatment with recombinant EPO increases stimulation of the bone marrow and therefore increases the rate of red blood cell production. However, producing more red blood cells requires more iron for haemoglobin synthesis. If iron stores are not adequately replaced, the increased demand for iron can eventually exceed available iron. This produces iron deficiency, causing red blood cells to become microcytic and hypochromic.

4 Parkinsonism

Answer: a. Antagonism of muscarinic acetylcholine receptors

Haloperidol is a high-potency D2 antagonist and blocking D2 receptors in the nigrostriatal pathway produces a functional dopamin deficit in the striatum, even though the dopaminergic neurons themselves are intact (DaT scan negative). In the striatum, dopamine normally inhibits cholinergic interneurons through D2 receptors (look up direct and indirect basal ganglia pathways). When D2 is blocked, that inhibition is lost and cholinergic activity is relatively excessive.

The symptoms come from too much cholinergic signalling relative to dopamine and a centrally acting anticholinergic (like procyclidine) does this by blocking muscarinic receptors in the basal ganglia. This doesn’t interfere with the antipsychotic effect like levodopa or bromocriptine would (b. and c.). Propanalol (d.) is used for akathisia (akathisia has a different mechanism to D2 blockade which we don’t really have a concrete answer for). Diazepam (e.) is used for acute dystonia, agitation or catatonia.

The goal of this question was to test this topic without the pattern recognition of identifying procyclidine/benztropine immediately as the treatment for drug-induced Parkinsonism.
