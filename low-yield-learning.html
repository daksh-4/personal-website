IN PROGRESS

Whether it be the Odyssey or the Vedas, it was the job of scholars to memorise epic tales, passed down generation to generation through word of mouth. Very few could read and write so information could be lost in a generation if not preserved appropriately. Now vast quantities of information are organised and available 24/7. While the way we access content has changed, the bones of good learning practices are the same. Spaced repetition and active recall.

  1. Read a fact or perform a task for the first time.
  2. Wait some time
  3. Do it again, trying to recreate what you did or read the first time from memory.
  4. Inevitably fail but see what you were missing.
  5. Back to step 2.

Broadly this calls on three states? (not sure if states is the right word). Recalling, waiting and reviewing. And at any point when trying to learn we are in one of these states whether consciously or subconsciously. Probably need something technical here. Side note, machines learn in the same way! Could talk about reinforcement learning, markov decision processes.

This seems almost too obvious to deserve an essay. And yet an enormous industry has grown around trying to make learning faster. Books and courses that vary just enough between them to leave you dissatisfied and still looking for a magic bullet. Is this even something worth chasing?

An Affliction of Optimisation

There is an idea that learning is something to be optimised and discretised. Are you a visual learner or a kinesthetic learner? (this concept of learning types has been thoroughly disproven ref) In my opinion, any one way of learning can be just as good as another so long as it incorporates the three steps of recalling, waiting and reviewing. In fact the thing that ended up improving the rate of learning was probably to do with becoming more intentional with the process and paying attention to what is in front of you.

Trying to optimise learning is counter-intuitive after a certain point. The process should be difficult, you are meant to keep forgetting things and for something to truly “stick” you sometimes have to keep reading the same sentence over and over again. Trying to remember everything you read once is simply not realistic and any time spent chasing this could have been spent doing other things. In my very anecdotal, non scientific wishy washy experience you probably retain somewhere between 10 to 70 percent of what you learn depending on the type of content (and the confidence interval on this is embarrassingly large).

What is the point of learning this thing if eventually I am just going to forget it? I love talking and thinking about this. Again very much straying into the realm of anecdotal evidence, but there is an intangible feeling I get when I understand a new concept for the first time. As if it has unlocked an extra dimension of the world I didn’t have before. A new connection has formed between ideas that were previously unrelated. Perhaps that is what I actually mean when I say that I have learned something. Not that I have successfully stored another fact in my head, but that I have acquired another way of looking at things. And the details will fade, but the appreciation does not. Like a map, I may forget the name of a town I once visited, but the shape of the landscape remains. I’d like to give you an example here but I suspect some of the most important things I have learnt are things I could no longer explain particularly well. They have been absorbed into the way I think. Tied into the web of assumptions, intuitions, analogies and connections rather than discrete pieces of information. And when you revisit a connection or add something new – plucking a strand of information – the adjacent section of webbing resonates. Consolidating ideas requires you to revisit the surrounding context, providing a positive feedback loop of recollection. I think maybe if I was too concerned with my retention percentages I might miss this.
